From vectors to constrained variations

Why the tangent matters

A functional assigns a number to a whole function: J measures its objective value, while K measures the constrained quantity. An allowed first-order variation changes y without changing K, so δK[η] = 0. In the coefficient picture, this is ∇K · t = 0: the tangent is perpendicular to the constraint gradient.

At stationarity, J must also have zero first-order change along every allowed tangent. Its gradient therefore has no tangent component. For a normal one-constraint problem, it must be a scalar multiple of the constraint gradient.

What the multiplier can cancel

With the article’s convention J + λ(K − k₀), the balance is ∇J + λ∇K = 0. Thus the amber arrow is −λ∇K. In the convention ∇J = μ∇K, the equivalent multiplier is μ = −λ; a negative λ is not a failed solution.

The normal-fit value cancels the part of ∇J parallel to ∇K. If a tangent component remains, no choice of this single scalar can remove it. That is why matching a multiplier away from a stationary curve does not solve the constrained problem.

Vectors and density functions

The upper arrows are derivatives with respect to the two coefficients a and b. The lower gJ and gK are functions of x representing the first variations after integration by parts. They describe the same stationarity condition in different representations; the coefficient arrows are not point samples of those density curves.

The L² residual is the square root of ∫(gJ + λgK)2 dx. Zero means the full density balance holds. The polynomial basis is not orthonormal, so this norm need not equal the coefficient-vector residual shown above.
